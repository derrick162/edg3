# Edge — ICP Target Profiles + Validation Plan
_Draft v1, June 2026. For Derrick's morning review. Feeds beta-readiness.md + design-partner-profiles.md._

---

## Why this document exists

Before we invite design partners, we need to know which user we're designing for — and what we're trying to learn from them. These aren't marketing personas. They're research hypotheses. Each ICP comes with a set of questions we don't yet know the answer to, and design partners are how we answer them.

The mission is constant across all ICPs: **prevent burnout for high-performers.** The wedge, the trust threshold, and the "aha" moment differ.

---

## ICP 1 — The ADHD Founder / Operator

### Profile
- **Who:** Founder, solo operator, or senior IC with diagnosed or self-identified ADHD. Age 28–42.
- **Company stage:** Early-stage startup (pre-seed to Series A), freelance, or high-autonomy role at a fast-moving company.
- **Daily context:** Calendar is chaotic — half-planned, half-reactive. Meetings booked in odd slots. Deep work blocks that never actually protect deep work. Starts the day with good intentions; by 11am is in reactive mode.
- **Tools they live in:** Google Calendar, Notion or Linear for task lists (often abandoned), Slack (constant), maybe Todoist or Things (also often abandoned). Every system eventually fails them.
- **How they currently cope:** hyperfocus sprints followed by crashes. Coffee. Late nights. Compensating with raw output for what they lose to distraction and decision fatigue.

### The pain
- **Calendar:** time blindness — meetings appear without warning; the day disappears. Difficulty estimating how long things take. No buffer between tasks.
- **Email/inbox:** gets buried fast. Urgent things mix with noise. Responding to email feels like a crisis every time.
- **Energy:** irregular. High-output days followed by complete depletion. Can't predict their own capacity.
- **Focus:** the hardest problem. Prioritization requires sustained executive function they don't always have. Every tool that asks "what do you want to work on?" becomes a source of friction.
- **What a bad day looks like:** woke up with a plan. Three urgent Slacks by 9am. Calendar has four back-to-back meetings. The one thing that mattered didn't happen. Went to bed feeling like they failed, again.
- **Where burnout shows up:** not in one dramatic crash — in the accumulation of "I know what I should be doing and I can't get to it." Identity erosion from constant underperformance relative to intention.

### Why Edge fits them
- **The "Edge tells you your focus" feature is the wedge.** The hardest ADHD problem is deciding what to do. Edge removes that decision entirely — it tells you, every morning, what your 3 areas of focus are for today. You just confirm. The cognitive load is gone.
- **"Do as little as possible, I'll handle the calendar"** — this is the ADHD-native design principle. The less the user has to initiate, the better. Edge calls them; they don't call Edge. Edge proposes; they say yes or no.
- **Voice-first removes friction.** ADHD users struggle with interfaces that require sustained attention. A 3-minute conversation is easier than opening an app.
- **The Momentum score creates the right accountability.** Not punishing missed days — just a visible streak that makes showing up feel good.

### Hypotheses
1. **Acute daily pain.** ADHD focus/priority pain is felt every single day — not occasionally. High confidence.
2. **Trust threshold is lower than expected.** ADHD users are pragmatic about tools. If it works, they'll give it almost anything. The barrier isn't "do I trust AI" — it's "will this actually stick." Medium confidence.
3. **Calendar access is less scary than email access.** Calendar is already shared everywhere. Gmail feels more private. May need to phase the ask (calendar first, Gmail optional). Medium confidence.
4. **They've tried 10 other tools.** Skepticism is real but latent — they want to believe this one is different. The first call needs to feel noticeably different from day 1. High confidence.
5. **Willingness to pay is high if it sticks.** They've spent money on coaches, therapists, apps. If Edge delivers, $49–79/mo is easy. But "if it sticks" is the condition. High confidence.

### Key questions to answer
1. Does the "Edge tells you your focus" moment actually land as relief, or does it feel like another thing to manage?
2. Does the morning call fit their routine — or do they resent being called at a fixed time?
3. What's the failure mode? When do they stop picking up?
4. Does the Momentum score motivate or shame?
5. At what point do they say "this is different from other tools"? What caused it?
6. Will they grant Gmail access, or does that feel like a line?

### Success signals
- Picks up calls ≥4/5 days in week 2
- Unprompted tells someone else about Edge within 30 days
- Explicitly says "I would have missed X without Edge" at least once
- Renews (or commits to paying) after free trial

## ICP 2 — The Burnt-Out Senior Executive / Operator

### Profile
- **Who:** Director, VP, or C-suite at a mid-to-large company, or a founder of a scaled startup. Age 35–50.
- **Company stage:** Growth-stage or established. Not a zero-to-one builder — a scaling operator.
- **Daily context:** Calendar is permanently over-full. 5–8 hours of meetings per day. Inbox has hundreds of unread emails. Makes 30+ decisions a day. Manages a team. Personal priorities (health, family, finance) consistently lose to work.
- **Tools they live in:** Google Calendar or Outlook, Slack or Teams, email. Executive assistant (sometimes). Possibly an EA they've outgrown.
- **How they currently cope:** they don't. They push through. Sunday nights are anxiety. They've told themselves this is temporary for three years.

### The pain
- **Calendar:** no control. Meetings get booked by others. Their day is owned by everyone else's needs. Deep work is theoretical.
- **Email:** existential. Hundreds of threads. Urgent things get missed. They've stopped believing their inbox reflects reality.
- **Energy:** depleted. Sleep is compromised. Recovery is an afterthought. They know they're declining but don't know how to stop.
- **Focus:** they haven't thought about their personal priorities in months. The company's priorities are their priorities by default.
- **What a bad day looks like:** six hours of meetings, none of which moved anything important. Responded to 40 emails; missed the three that mattered. End of day: exhausted and behind.
- **Where burnout shows up:** emotional flatness. Cynicism. Physical symptoms (sleep disruption, frequent illness). The slow disappearance of the person they were before the job consumed them.

### Why Edge fits them
- **The hero loop is the wedge.** This user needs someone to look at their week and say "this is wrong, here's why, I fixed it." Edge does exactly that. They can't restructure their own calendar — they've tried. But they'll approve a specific, credible proposal in 30 seconds.
- **Whoop energy integration is high-value.** This segment tends to be health-aware (they often have Whoops or Ouras) and receptive to data-driven recovery guidance. Edge connecting energy to scheduling is something their EA can't do.
- **The Edge Score gives them a language for something they feel but can't measure.** "My focus score is 38" cuts through denial faster than "I'm tired."
- **Trust via receipts.** High-seniority users are control-conscious. The Activity tab, expandable inbox receipts, and fact correction give them visibility into exactly what Edge did — which is what makes delegation feel safe.

### Hypotheses
1. **Pain is real but suppressed.** They've normalized the dysfunction. Edge may need to surface the problem before proposing the solution. "Your calendar shows 3h of deep work scheduled and 0h actually protected — here's what that cost you this week." Medium-high confidence.
2. **Trust threshold is HIGH.** This segment has more to lose. Handing their calendar to an AI feels different when they're managing a board, investors, or a large team. The trust infrastructure matters a lot here. High confidence.
3. **Gmail access will be the sticking point.** Corporate email + AI = risk-conscious. Some will decline Gmail entirely. Edge should be clearly valuable without it. High confidence.
4. **Willingness to pay is highest here.** $79–99/mo is nothing compared to the value of one additional high-quality hour per day. But ROI must be felt quickly. High confidence.
5. **Retention risk is high if the first week is rough.** Senior operators have no patience for broken UX. If the onboarding fails or the first call is off, they won't come back. High confidence.

### Key questions to answer
1. Does the hero loop "wow" moment land fast enough — within the first call?
2. What's the trust barrier for Gmail specifically? Is metadata-only enough, or do they want to opt out entirely?
3. Does the Whoop energy + scheduling integration feel like a differentiator, or a gimmick?
4. What level of calendar control do they want to retain? Does one-click approval feel right, or too fast?
5. What would make them recommend Edge to a peer? What would make them embarrassed to have used it?
6. How do they feel about the morning call format — does it fit, or does it conflict with standing meetings?

### Success signals
- Stays on past week 2 (high churn risk in first 14 days)
- Connects Whoop within first week
- Approves ≥1 hero loop reshape in first 5 calls
- Refers one peer within 60 days

## ICP 3 — The Early-Stage Solo Founder (Context-Switch Hell)

### Profile
- **Who:** Founder of a 0–5 person startup, pre-product-market-fit. Solo or with one co-founder. Age 25–38.
- **Company stage:** Idea → seed. Building and selling simultaneously. No EA, no chief of staff, no process.
- **Daily context:** switches between product, sales, ops, hiring, and investor outreach in a single morning. Every task is "urgent." Calendar is half-empty but the day is completely reactive.
- **Tools they live in:** Google Calendar, Gmail, Notion, Linear, Slack. Chrome tabs. Voice memos. The notes app.
- **How they currently cope:** sheer will and caffeine. Works long hours to compensate for inefficiency. Dreams about having an EA. Reads productivity books and feels guilty for not implementing them.

### The pain
- **Context-switching:** the defining pain. Moves between investor email, product decision, customer call, and team sync in 2-hour windows. Each switch costs 20+ minutes of re-orientation. By 3pm, nothing substantial has been done.
- **Focus:** no mechanism for protecting focus. Knows what matters; can't get to it. Every system tried eventually collapsed under the chaos.
- **Email:** signal-to-noise is brutal. A financial notice sits next to a newsletter. An urgent investor reply gets missed.
- **Energy:** irregular. High highs, rough lows. Doesn't track it; feels it viscerally.
- **What a bad day looks like:** nine hours of work, nothing shipped, three fires put out. Goes to bed feeling like a reactive machine, not a founder.
- **Where burnout shows up:** early. This is the segment most at risk of burning out before they even hit product-market-fit. The urgency spiral — everything feels equally urgent — is the precursor.

### Why Edge fits them
- **Open loops.** This user's head is full of open loops — commitments made on calls, things to follow up on, things they said they'd decide. Edge surfaces these every morning. For a solo founder, this is like getting their working memory back.
- **Focus recommendation.** The most valuable thing Edge can tell a solo founder is: "of everything you could work on today, here are your 3 areas of focus." That's the decision they most need help making and most often defer.
- **Email signal.** Financial and legal threads getting prioritized automatically is high-value — solo founders often have unpleasant surprises from missed important emails.
- **The call itself is the ritual.** Solo founders have no accountability structure. The morning call creates one: a 3-minute forcing function to actually think about the day before reacting to it.

### Hypotheses
1. **The pain is acute and daily.** Context-switching cost is felt constantly. High confidence.
2. **Trust threshold is lower than executives.** Solo founders are early adopters by definition. Less institutional risk. High confidence.
3. **Gmail access is likely fine.** Solo founders live in Gmail. Less corporate anxiety. Medium-high confidence.
4. **Willingness to pay is the question.** Runway-conscious. May need a clear value demonstration before committing to $49/mo. Trial period matters more here. Medium confidence.
5. **Retention is usage-dependent.** If Edge doesn't help in week 1, they'll quietly stop. No obligation to stay polite. Medium confidence.

### Key questions to answer
1. Does open loops surfacing actually reduce cognitive load, or does it add anxiety?
2. Does the morning call create accountability, or does the fixed call time feel like another obligation?
3. Is $49/mo defensible for a pre-revenue founder? What would make it an obvious yes?
4. Which feature makes them say "I would have missed that without Edge"?
5. Do they want Edge to be more aggressive (propose more, do more) or more gentle (ask more, propose less)?

### Success signals
- Describes Edge to someone else as "my morning briefing" (identity adoption)
- Explicitly credits Edge with surfacing something they would have missed
- Stays on past 30 days despite tight budget

## ICP 4 — The VC-Backed Founder (Runway + Energy Pressure)

### Profile
- **Who:** Founder of a venture-backed startup, Series A–B. Has a team (5–50 people). Reports to a board.
- **Company stage:** Scaling. Burn rate is real. Pressure is external (investors, board, competitors) and internal (team culture, retention, product velocity).
- **Daily context:** calendar is shared and political — too much on it signals weak prioritization; too little signals low hustle. Manages up (board) and down (team) simultaneously. Has an EA or is about to hire one.
- **Tools they live in:** Google Workspace (Calendar + Gmail), Notion or Confluence, Slack, Linear. Monthly board reporting. Quarterly OKRs they half-believe in.
- **How they currently cope:** OKRs, a chief of staff if they can afford it, Sunday planning rituals that erode under Monday pressure, and sheer force of will.

### The pain
- **Strategic drift:** the calendar fills with team meetings and fires. The work that actually matters — product strategy, key hires, important customer relationships — gets squeezed to the margins.
- **Energy management:** running on adrenaline and accountability. No real recovery rhythm. Knows they're running hot. Afraid to admit it.
- **Focus:** pulled in too many directions by too many legitimate priorities. The hard thing isn't motivation — it's triage.
- **Runway pressure:** every week that passes without product-market-fit or revenue growth is a week closer to a difficult board conversation. This creates a specific anxiety that colors every priority decision.
- **What a bad day looks like:** four team syncs, two investor updates, one customer escalation. Nothing shipped. Their CEO scorecard, if they had one, would be all red.
- **Where burnout shows up:** detachment. The founder who used to be energized by the product becomes a manager of processes. The product stops being their creative work and starts being their job.

### Why Edge fits them
- **Focus recommendation tied to runway.** Edge can be explicitly calibrated to "extend runway" as a top-level anchor. For a VC-backed founder, Edge becoming the thing that keeps strategic priorities from being buried by operational noise is high-value.
- **Hero loop for calendar hygiene.** Back-to-back meetings, no deep work blocks, over-scheduled low-recovery days — Edge identifies these and fixes them. This is what a chief of staff does. For founders who can't afford one yet, Edge is the substitute.
- **The Edge Score is their new metric.** A founder who tracks NRR and burn rate will track their Edge Score. It becomes the leading indicator for "am I still running the company or is the company running me?"
- **Trust via audit trail.** Board-aware founders care about who can see what. The Activity log, encrypted storage, and user-scoped data give them the visibility and control they need to feel safe.

### Hypotheses
1. **Pain is real but they've intellectualized it.** They can articulate the problem clearly but haven't felt the urgency to solve it (too busy solving other problems). Edge needs to demonstrate value, not just describe it. Medium-high confidence.
2. **Trust threshold is similar to executives.** Institutional caution + "what does this look like to my board/investors." High confidence.
3. **Willingness to pay is high if the ROI narrative is clear.** "This is cheaper than one hour of my time per week" — if Edge delivers more than one genuinely better hour per week, it pays for itself. High confidence.
4. **The EA comparison is both the opportunity and the risk.** If Edge is framed as an EA substitute, it sets a high bar. If it's framed as a complement (EA for logistics, Edge for focus + energy), the bar is more manageable. Medium confidence.
5. **Referral rate is high if the product works.** VC-backed founders talk to each other constantly. One strong convert in this segment = multiple warm referrals. High confidence.

### Key questions to answer
1. Does Edge actually protect strategic time from operational drag, or does it just move the same blocks around?
2. How does the board / investor dynamic affect their comfort with AI calendar access?
3. Is the morning call format compatible with their actual morning (often team standups, early investor calls)?
4. Would they use Edge alongside an EA, or does it replace the need for one?
5. What's the referral trigger — what would make them proactively tell a peer founder to try Edge?

### Success signals
- Connects Whoop within week 1 (high health consciousness in this segment)
- Books one "strategic block" that previously didn't exist on their calendar in week 1
- References Edge in a 1:1 with a team member or investor ("Edge flagged this")
- Refers at least one other founder within 45 days

## Prioritized Beachhead Recommendation

### Win ICP 1 first: The ADHD Founder / Operator

**The case:**

1. **Founder-market fit is strongest here.** Derrick is this person. Every product decision made so far has been grounded in this exact pain. The product hasn't been theorized for this segment — it's been built from the inside.

2. **The pain is most acute and most daily.** ADHD focus/priority pain doesn't take weekends off. The urgency to use Edge is present every single morning. Other ICPs have the pain; ICP 1 *feels* it most acutely.

3. **Trust threshold is lowest.** ADHD users are pragmatic about tools that work. Less institutional risk, more personal openness. The trust features matter — but less persuasion is needed to get them to try.

4. **Word-of-mouth velocity is highest.** ADHD communities are tight-knit and vocal. A product that genuinely works gets shared fast. One strong convert in this segment ripples.

5. **The product's core design principle is ADHD-native.** "Do as little as possible, I'll handle the calendar." Voice-first. Edge proposes, you confirm. This design was right for everyone but it's *perfect* for ICP 1.

6. **The beachhead is defensible.** Once Edge is known as the tool for ADHD high-performers, competitors can't easily copy the positioning. It's rooted in authentic founder-market fit, not a marketing claim.

**The sequence:**
- Win ICP 1 (ADHD founders/operators) in design partner phase
- Use ICP 1 signal to refine the product and testimonials
- Expand to ICP 2 (burnt-out executives) in wave 2 — same pain, higher trust bar, larger segment
- ICP 3 (solo founders) and ICP 4 (VC-backed founders) are adjacent and addressable in wave 3

**What winning ICP 1 looks like:**
- 3 of 5 design partners from ICP 1 profile
- At least one unprompted referral from an ADHD community (Reddit, Discord, Twitter)
- At least one "this is the first tool that's actually worked for me" testimonial
